# Mental Models Toolkit

The idea, from Charlie Munger: don't rely on one way of looking at a problem. Carry a "latticework" of a few dozen big ideas and use the ones that fit. This file is the short list that matters most for running and growing a distributor like The O-Ring Store. Each model has what it is, the question to ask, and how it shows up at TORS.

## 1. First Principles Thinking (the foundation)

**What it is.** Break a problem down to the things you know are true, then build back up from there instead of reasoning by comparison ("everybody else does it this way"). Goes back to Aristotle, who called a first principle "the first basis from which a thing is known." Elon Musk's battery example is the famous modern one: batteries were "expensive" until he priced out the raw materials and found they cost a fraction of the finished pack.

**The method (use this every time):**

1. **State the goal or problem plainly.** One sentence.
2. **List every assumption** hiding in it. Write them all down, even the obvious ones.
3. **Sort each assumption:**
   - *Physics*: true no matter what (cash has to come in before or alongside cash going out; a part we don't have can't ship today).
   - *Checkable fact*: could be true or false, and we can look it up (our turns are X, customers buy because of Y).
   - *Convention*: true only because that's how it's been done (we need a sales rep per territory, we have to stock everything a customer might want).
4. **Challenge with Socratic questions** on anything that isn't physics:
   - Why do I believe this? Where did it come from?
   - What's the evidence? What would prove it wrong?
   - What if the opposite were true?
   - What happens if we just don't do it?
5. **Rebuild from what's left.** With only physics and verified facts, what's the simplest way to get the outcome?

**At TORS:** "Capital is what's holding us back" is a belief, not physics. First principles asks: capital for what, exactly? If inventory, which inventory, and what does a dollar of it return? Is there cash tied up today that isn't working? Is the bottleneck really what we can stock, or how many people find us, or how fast we can quote and ship?

**Five Whys** is the quick version: keep asking "why?" until you hit something that isn't explained by something else. Usually takes 3 to 5 rounds.

## 2. Theory of Constraints (the most important model for this work)

**What it is.** Eliyahu Goldratt (*The Goal*, 1984). Every system has one constraint that limits how much it can produce. Improving anything other than the constraint is a mirage. An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is worth nothing; an hour lost at the bottleneck is lost for the whole business.

**The Five Focusing Steps:**

1. **Identify** the constraint. The one thing that, if it doubled, would make the whole business grow.
2. **Exploit** it. Get the most out of it with what you already have before spending money.
3. **Subordinate** everything else to it. Every other part of the business works to keep the constraint fed and never idle, even if that makes the other parts look "inefficient."
4. **Elevate** it. Now spend money: hire, buy, borrow, build.
5. **Repeat.** Once it's broken, the constraint moves somewhere else. Don't let inertia become the new constraint.

**Types of constraints:**
- *Market*: not enough demand reaching us (people don't know we exist, or don't pick us)
- *Capacity*: we can't process more (quoting, picking, shipping, kitting, custom work)
- *Supply*: vendors, lead times, minimums
- *Capital / cash*: can't fund inventory or growth
- *People / skill*: not enough of the right people, or one person everything flows through
- *Policy*: a rule, habit, or belief ("we don't do X") that limits us. Goldratt said these are the most common and the hardest to see.

**TOC money language** (simpler than accounting):
- **Throughput** = sales minus truly variable cost (for us, roughly gross margin dollars). Money coming in.
- **Inventory / Investment** = money tied up in things we plan to sell.
- **Operating Expense** = money spent turning inventory into throughput.
- Goal: raise throughput while lowering investment and holding expense. Goldratt's point is throughput has no ceiling, while cutting cost and inventory does.

**At TORS:** The interview's job is step 1. Is the constraint market (finding customers), capacity (people and process), capital (inventory funding), or a policy we haven't noticed?

## 3. Inversion

**What it is.** "Invert, always invert" (Carl Jacobi, by way of Munger). Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how you'd guarantee failure, then don't do those things.

**Question:** What would make sure we never get to $10M? What would kill the business if Marty were gone for six months?

**At TORS:** Great for the succession side. List what breaks if Marty, Daro, or Darion is out. That list is the administration plan.

## 4. Second-Order Thinking

**What it is.** Ask "and then what?" First-order effects are obvious. Second- and third-order effects are where the surprises are.

**Question:** If this works, what happens next? Who reacts, and how?

**At TORS:** Borrowing to load up on stock: first order, better fill rate. Second order, interest cost and cash pressure if it doesn't turn. Third order, slow stock gets written down and the lender wants covenants. Doesn't mean don't do it. Means know the chain.

## 5. Opportunity Cost

**What it is.** The real cost of anything is the best thing you gave up to do it.

**Question:** If we spend this dollar or this hour here, what are we not doing?

**At TORS:** A dollar sitting in a part that hasn't sold in a year is a dollar not sitting in a part that turns six times. Same with Marty's hours.

## 6. Pareto (80/20)

**What it is.** A small share of causes drive most of the results. Usually not exactly 80/20, but lopsided.

**Question:** Which 20% of customers, SKUs, vendors, or activities make 80% of the profit? Which make 80% of the headaches?

**At TORS:** Run it on SKUs (turns and margin), customers (margin dollars, not sales), and time.

## 7. Circle of Competence

**What it is.** Know what you're actually great at, stay inside it or grow it on purpose. The edge of the circle is where expensive mistakes happen.

**Question:** What do we do better than McMaster, Global O-Ring, Apple Rubber, and the big boys? Where are we out of our depth?

**At TORS:** Speed, hard-to-find sizes, small-quantity orders, service, ISO 9001 quality, custom work. Is growth coming from inside the circle or outside it?

## 8. Margin of Safety

**What it is.** From Ben Graham. Build in room for being wrong. Engineers design a bridge for more weight than it'll ever carry.

**Question:** If this goes half as well as we hope, are we still OK?

**At TORS:** Cash reserves before taking on debt, second suppliers on key lines, cross-trained people.

## 9. Map Is Not the Territory

**What it is.** Reports, dashboards, and plans are maps. The business is the territory. Maps are useful and always leave things out.

**Question:** What's going on that the numbers don't show?

**At TORS:** NetSuite shows orders. It doesn't show the customers who called, couldn't get what they needed, and went elsewhere. Lost demand is invisible on the map.

## 10. Probabilistic Thinking and Expected Value

**What it is.** The future is a range of outcomes, not a single forecast. Weigh each by how likely it is.

**Question:** Best case, worst case, most likely? What are the odds of each?

**At TORS:** Use for big bets: new product lines, hires, acquisitions, loans.

## 11. One-Way vs Two-Way Doors

**What it is.** Jeff Bezos. Two-way doors can be walked back through cheaply, so decide fast. One-way doors can't, so slow down.

**At TORS:** A new marketing test is a two-way door. A 10-year lease or big debt is a one-way door.

## 12. Incentives

**What it is.** "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome." (Munger.) People do what they're rewarded for, not what they're told.

**Question:** What is each person rewarded for today? Does that line up with the constraint?

## 13. Occam's Razor and Hanlon's Razor

- **Occam:** The simplest explanation that fits the facts is usually right. Start there.
- **Hanlon:** Don't blame on bad intent what's explained by a broken process or missing info. Fix the process.

## 14. Regret Minimization

**What it is.** Bezos again. Project yourself to 80 and ask which choice you'd regret not making.

**At TORS:** Good for the personal goals (land, lake, vacations) and the "work until I decide I'm done" question.

## 15. Pre-Mortem

**What it is.** Gary Klein. Before starting, imagine it's a year from now and the plan failed. Write down why. Then fix those things now.

**At TORS:** Run one on each of the four plans before calling them finished.

## 16. Leverage and Compounding

**What it is.** Small edges repeated add up. Leverage is getting more output per unit of input (capital, people, code, content, automation).

**At TORS:** Automation and AI are leverage on people. Content and SEO compound. Repeat customers compound.

## Distributor math that keeps us honest

These come up constantly in the interview. Rough industry numbers from vendor sources, so treat them as orientation and compare against our own trend.

- **Inventory turns** = annual cost of goods sold / average inventory at cost. Industrial / MRO distributors often run roughly 3 to 6 turns; spare-parts businesses that promise "we have it" can run 2 to 3 on purpose.
- **GMROI** (gross margin return on inventory) = annual gross margin dollars / average inventory at cost. Every dollar of stock returns this many dollars of margin a year. Above 2 is generally considered healthy.
- **Days of inventory** = 365 / turns.
- **Cash conversion cycle** = days of inventory + days to collect from customers minus days to pay vendors. The shorter it is, the less capital growth eats.
- **Capital needed to grow** (rough): extra sales x (1 minus gross margin %) / turns. So at 60% margin and 1.4 turns, every extra $1M in sales needs about $285K more inventory. At 3 turns, it needs about $135K. Faster turns cut the capital you need in half.
